# The Madness of Markets (Alex Edmans): Research Papers by Section

A per-section guide to the research the book discusses. Author names come from the text or the book's endnotes. If the book doesn't name the authors, the entry says *(authors not named)*. Books, press articles and industry reports are only included when the text uses them as evidence.

## Introduction

- **Edmans, García & Norli (2007)**: *Topic:* whether national football results affect stock markets. *Finding:* stock markets consistently fall after a country's national team loses a match (750 games, 39 countries).
- **Dimson, Marsh & Staunton** (*Triumph of the Optimists*, updated): *Topic:* long-run returns on equities vs. Treasury bills. *Finding:* $1 invested in US stocks in 1900 grew to ~$125,000 by 2025, against ~$69 in T-bills.

## Part I: Overreaction

### Chapter 1: Bubble Trouble: Why We All Go Crazy at the Top

#### Opening: psychology of overreaction

- **Kahneman & Tversky (1974)**: *Topic:* anchoring heuristic. *Finding:* people estimating 8×7×…×1 guess far higher (2,250) than those seeing 1×2×…×8 (512), because they anchor on the first numbers.
- **Tversky & Kahneman (1971)**: *Topic:* the "law of small numbers" / overextrapolation. *Finding:* people rate stockbrokers as highly skilled after a single strong final year, ignoring an otherwise mediocre record.
- **Shiller (1981)**: *Topic:* excess volatility. *Finding:* stock prices move far more than later changes in dividends can justify.
- **Greenwood & Shleifer (2014)**: *Topic:* investor return expectations from surveys. *Finding:* investors expect high returns after markets rise and low returns after they fall, the opposite of what actually happens to expected returns.

#### How the Worst Become First

- **De Bondt & Thaler (1985)**: *Topic:* long-term return reversal. *Finding:* 3-year "loser" stocks beat the market by 20% over the next three years while "winners" underperformed by 5%.
- **Blackburn & Cakici (2017)**: *Topic:* international reversal. *Finding:* the reversal effect holds in at least 22 other countries.
- **Khang & King (2004)**: *Topic:* reversal in bonds. *Finding:* return reversals also appear in the bond market.
- **Frankel & Rose (1996)**: *Topic:* reversal in currencies. *Finding:* real exchange rates mean-revert.
- **Lubnau & Todorova (2015)**: *Topic:* reversal in energy futures. *Finding:* mean-reversion strategies are profitable in energy futures.
- **Bhojraj & Swaminathan (2006)**: *Topic:* country-level reversal. *Finding:* buying the worst-performing national markets and selling the best earns profits.
- **Zaremba, Kizys & Raza (2020)**: *Topic:* long-run reversal across 71 countries, 1830–2019. *Finding:* buying loser countries and selling winners earned 4.5% per year.
- **Zaremba, Bianchi & Mikutowski (2021)**: *Topic:* reversal in commodities, 1265–2017. *Finding:* the reversal strategy delivered about 13% per year over seven centuries.
- **McLean & Pontiff (2016)**: *Topic:* whether academic publication kills anomalies. *Finding:* across 97 strategies, returns drop by only about one-third after publication.
- **Jacobs & Müller (2020)**: *Topic:* post-publication anomaly returns internationally. *Finding:* across 39 countries, returns do not decline after publication.
- **Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020); Tobek & Hronec (2021)**: *Topic:* machine learning in asset pricing. *Finding:* using ML to combine many anomaly signals beats exploiting any one of them alone.
- **Cao, Jiang, Wang & Yang (2024)**: *Topic:* man vs. machine in stock analysis. *Finding:* AI combined with human analyst insight ("man + machine") does better than either alone.

#### Buying Boring

- **Lakonishok, Shleifer & Vishny (1994)**: *Topic:* value vs. glamour (contrarian investing). *Finding:* low-P/E value stocks beat high-P/E glamour stocks by 7.6% per year (11% per year using market-to-book).
- **Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013)**: *Topic:* value (and momentum) across asset classes. *Finding:* the value premium shows up in the UK, Europe, Japan, and in bonds, currencies and commodities.
- *(Greenblatt's "magic formula", later research, authors not named)*: *Topic:* profitability/quality. *Finding:* profitability predicts future stock returns, which supports combining value with quality.

#### Small Wins

- **Banz (1981)**: *Topic:* the size effect. *Finding:* small stocks beat large ones by more than 1% per month on a beta-adjusted basis, though the effect faded after publication.
- **Basu (1977)** (endnote): *Topic:* low-P/E stocks. *Finding:* low-P/E stocks outperformed, though the study did not control for risk.
- **Asness, Frazzini, Israel, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2018)**: *Topic:* "Size matters, if you control your junk". *Finding:* filtering out low-quality "junk" lifts the size premium from an insignificant 1.7% to 5.9% per year.

#### When Reason Fans the Flames

- **Brunnermeier & Nagel (2004)**: *Topic:* hedge funds during the dot-com bubble. *Finding:* hedge funds rode the tech bubble rather than shorting it, outperforming by 4.5% per quarter and exiting before the crash.
- **Shleifer & Vishny (1997)**: *Topic:* limits to arbitrage. *Finding:* arbitrage is risky and capital-constrained, so mispricing can last (e.g., Tiger Management was forced to liquidate just before the crash).
- **Chopra, Lakonishok & Ritter (1992)**: *Topic:* reversal in small stocks. *Finding:* the reversal effect is strongest among small stocks.
- **McLean (2010)**: *Topic:* idiosyncratic risk and reversal. *Finding:* reversal is more profitable in stocks with high idiosyncratic risk, where arbitrage is harder.

#### The Mirror that Moves the World

- **Luo (2005)**: *Topic:* managers learning from market reactions to M&A. *Finding:* across 2,000+ deals, the worse the market's reaction to an announced acquisition, the more likely it is to be cancelled.
- **Edmans, Goldstein & Jiang (2015)**: *Topic:* feedback effects and asymmetric trading. *Finding:* because prices influence real decisions, traders may hold back on trading against bad news, which limits arbitrage.

#### When Wall Street Hits Main Street

- **Bond, Edmans & Goldstein (2012); Goldstein (2023)**: *Topic:* the real effects of financial markets. *Finding:* stock prices shape corporate decisions even when no capital is raised, because managers learn from prices.
- **Polk & Sapienza (2009)**: *Topic:* catering theory of investment. *Finding:* overpriced firms invest more to pander to the market, especially when they are opaque or held by short-term investors.

### Chapter 2: Mood Music: How Feelings Cloud Finance

#### Opening: weather and returns

- **Saunders (1993)**: *Topic:* weather and stock returns. *Finding:* NYSE returns are higher on sunny days in New York (low cloud cover).
- **Hirshleifer & Shumway (2003)**: *Topic:* sunshine effect, out of sample. *Finding:* across 26 international exchanges, morning sunshine in the financial centre predicts higher daily index returns.

#### From Fun Fact to Fundamental Flaw

- **Cortés, Duchin & Sosyura (2016)**: *Topic:* weather-driven mood and mortgage lending. *Finding:* loan officers approve more (and riskier) mortgages on sunny days, and these loans default more often.

#### How Beckham Bends the Markets

- **Cho, Barnes & Guanara (2017)**: *Topic:* sleep loss and judicial decisions. *Finding:* judges hand down sentences about 5% longer on the "Sleepy Monday" after the daylight saving time shift.
- **Kamstra, Kramer & Levi** *(daylight-saving study, referenced in text)*: *Topic:* daylight saving time and markets. *Finding:* returns dip on the Monday after clocks change (US, Canada, UK; not Germany).
- **Kamstra, Kramer & Levi (2003)**: *Topic:* seasonal affective disorder (SAD). *Finding:* markets underperform before the winter solstice and outperform after it, most strongly far from the equator.
- **Eren & Mocan (2018)**: *Topic:* emotional judges. *Finding:* after unexpected Louisiana State football losses, local judges (especially alumni) give harsher sentences to juveniles.
- *(Ohio State lottery study, authors not named)*: *Topic:* sports wins and risk-taking. *Finding:* lottery sales in Ohio rise after Ohio State football wins.
- **Edmans, García & Norli (2007)**: *Topic:* sports sentiment and stock returns. *Finding:* national market returns drop after international football losses (also cricket, rugby and basketball, but not ice hockey), with no matching gain after wins, and the effect is larger for small stocks.
- **Kaplanski & Levy (2010)**: *Topic:* FIFA World Cup effect on US stocks. *Finding:* the US market averaged −2.6% during World Cups vs. +1.2% on the same dates in other years.

#### The Soundtrack of Sentiment

- **Edmans, Fernandez-Perez, Garel & Indriawan (2022)**: *Topic:* music sentiment (Spotify song valence) as a measure of national mood. *Finding:* happier music in a week predicts higher stock returns the next week (40 countries), followed by a reversal, plus fund inflows and lower bond prices.
- **Sadka**: *Topic:* firm-level sensitivity to music sentiment. *Finding:* going long mood-sensitive stocks and short insensitive ones earns about 2% per year.

#### The Hormones of Hedge Funds

- **Coates (and team)**: *Topic:* trader hormones and risk-taking. *Finding:* morning testosterone spikes predict more afternoon risk-taking, while cortisol rises with volatility and suppresses risk-taking ("the biology of bubble and crash").
- **Edmans, Flammer & Glossner (2024)**: *Topic:* diversity vs. inclusion and firm performance. *Finding:* inclusion is more strongly linked to financial performance than demographic diversity.
- **Rozenblit & Keil (2002)**: *Topic:* the illusion of explanatory depth. *Finding:* when asked to explain how everyday things work, people realise they know less than they thought and lower their confidence.

### Chapter 3: Much Ado About Nothing: The Name-Game and Other Non-News

#### Opening: EntreMed

- **Huberman & Regev (2001)**: *Topic:* stale news and contagious speculation. *Finding:* EntreMed's stock rose sevenfold after a front-page NYT story that repeated news published five months earlier.
- **Fedyk (2023)**: *Topic:* news positioning on Bloomberg terminals. *Finding:* front-page news is priced within an hour, while equally important non-front-page news takes over two days.

#### Surprised by the Unsurprising

- **Gilbert, Kogan, Lochstoer & Ozyildirim (2012)**: *Topic:* investor inattention to summary statistics (Leading Economic Index). *Finding:* markets react to the LEI even though its components are already public, then reverse, and trading on it earns about 8% per year.
- **Chang, Hartzmark, Solomon & Soltes (2017)**: *Topic:* earnings seasonality. *Finding:* firms with historically strong earnings in a given quarter earn higher returns when those predictable earnings are announced (4–7% per year long-short).
- **Heston & Sadka (2008)**: *Topic:* return seasonality. *Finding:* stocks with high returns in a given calendar month in past years tend to do well in that month again.
- **Aquino & Gershenson**: *Topic:* nostalgia in baseball card prices. *Finding:* a Hall-of-Famer's rookie card price jumps about 20% when he dies, even when his death was foreseeable.
- **Rashes (2001)**: *Topic:* ticker confusion (MCI vs. MCIC). *Finding:* investors mistakenly trade similarly named stocks, moving the wrong stock's price.

#### Forced Signals, False Alarms

- **Coval & Stafford (2007)**: *Topic:* mutual fund fire sales. *Finding:* funds facing large outflows dump their holdings, pushing prices down for reasons unrelated to fundamentals.
- **Edmans, Goldstein & Jiang (2012)**: *Topic:* real effects of prices on takeovers, using mutual-fund-flow-driven price pressure. *Finding:* fire-sale price drops take up to two years to recover, and buying the dumped stocks after two months earns about 7% by month 24.
- **Hau & Lai (2013)**: *Topic:* contagion through fund portfolios in the financial crisis. *Finding:* non-financial stocks held by funds heavily exposed to banks were sold off and underpriced "by association".

#### Category Error

- **Cooper, Dimitrov & Rau (2001)**: *Topic:* dot-com name changes. *Finding:* firms adding ".com", ".net" or "Internet" to their names saw stock jumps averaging 74%, even with no change in business.
- **Cooper, Gulen & Rau (2005)**: *Topic:* mutual fund name changes to hot styles. *Finding:* renamed funds got 28% more inflows but underperformed by 3% over the next year.
- **Gibson Brandon, Glossner, Krueger, Matos & Steffen (2022)**: *Topic:* UN PRI signatories. *Finding:* US funds that signed the PRI held stocks with lower ESG ratings than non-signatories.
- **Liang, Sun & Teo (2022)**: *Topic:* responsible hedge funds. *Finding:* hedge funds used the PRI label to attract flows and raise fees while weakening their ESG exposure.
- **Caquineau, Möttölä & Schumacher / Morningstar (2016)**: *Topic:* closet indexing in Europe. *Finding:* about a fifth of European large-cap "active" funds were closet indexers.
- **Cremers & Petajisto (2009)**: *Topic:* active share. *Finding:* funds with high active share outperform, even after fees.

#### Crossing the Line

- **Chen, Cohen & Lou (2016)**: *Topic:* industry window dressing (segment sales thresholds). *Finding:* firms bunch just above 50% sales in the higher-valued segment (often by cutting prices there), gain 1.4% on reclassification, then underperform.
- **Sloan (1996)**: *Topic:* the accruals anomaly. *Finding:* firms with the highest accruals underperform those with the lowest by over 10% the next year.
- **Bhojraj, Hribar, Picconi & McInnis (2009)**: *Topic:* firms that just beat or just miss analyst forecasts. *Finding:* "false beaters" (who hit targets through accruals or cuts) outperform "honest missers" briefly, then underperform by about 40% over three years.

#### Faking a Fat Dividend

- **Harris, Hartzmark & Solomon (2015)**: *Topic:* dividend "juicing" by mutual funds. *Finding:* 1 in 15 funds pays out more than twice the dividends its holdings justify, gets about 10% more inflows, and underperforms by more than 2% per year.

#### Yesterday's Heroes

- **Carhart (1997)**: *Topic:* persistence in mutual fund performance. *Finding:* a fund's past returns do not predict its future performance.
- **Berk & Green (2004)**: *Topic:* fund flows and performance in rational markets. *Finding:* even skilled managers fail to keep outperforming, because inflows chasing past success create diminishing returns to scale.
- **Morningstar "Mind the Gap" (2023)**: *Topic:* investor returns vs. fund returns. *Finding:* the average investor earned 6.0% vs. 7.7% for the average fund, because of badly timed flows.

### Chapter 4: Monkey See, Monkey Buy: Viral Herding in Markets

#### Opening: footballers, FTX and herding

- **Brown, Ivković, Smith & Weisbenner (2008)**: *Topic:* community effects on stock market participation. *Finding:* people are more likely to invest in stocks if their neighbours do, e.g., lifelong locals invest more when newcomers arrive from high-participation states.
- **Ivković & Weisbenner (2007)**: *Topic:* word-of-mouth in household stock purchases. *Finding:* households tend to buy the industries their neighbours are buying.
- **Hong, Kubik & Stein (2005)**: *Topic:* word-of-mouth among money managers. *Finding:* fund managers are more likely to buy or sell a stock when peers in the same city do.
- **Hilary & Hsu (2013)** *(cited for analyst herding)*: *Topic:* analyst forecast behaviour. *Finding:* analysts give rosier forecasts when other analysts already have.

#### Friends in High (and Far) Places

- **Bailey, Cao, Kuchler & Stroebel (2018)**: *Topic:* social networks and housing beliefs (Facebook). *Finding:* if distant friends' housing markets boom, people see their own area as a better investment, are more likely to buy, and buy bigger, pricier homes.
- **Gelman, Hirshleifer, Levi & Reiter Gavish (2024)**: *Topic:* social interaction intensity (local football games) and investing. *Finding:* local games lead fans to buy the same stocks, especially in similar communities and for risky stocks.

#### The Mindlessness of Strangers

- **Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer & Welch (1992)**: *Topic:* informational cascades. *Finding:* people rationally set aside their own information and copy others' earlier actions, which can lock in fads.
- **Asch (1951)**: *Topic:* conformity under group pressure. *Finding:* about a third of answers conformed to a clearly wrong unanimous group, and three-quarters of participants conformed at least once.
- **Kim, Lee & Kauffman (2023)**: *Topic:* Reddit sentiment and the GameStop squeeze. *Finding:* the tone and volume of Reddit posts predicted actual buying almost in real time.
- **Hirshleifer (2020)**: *Topic:* social transmission bias (ant "death spiral" analogy). *Finding:* ideas spread through social channels in biased ways that can lock groups into self-reinforcing errors.

#### Smoke (from) Signals

- **Artiga Gonzalez, Dyakov, Inhoffen & Wipplinger**: *Topic:* crowding among international mutual funds. *Finding:* across 17,000+ funds, those buying the same stocks as peers underperform index funds by 1.4% per year.
- **Pool, Stoffman & Yonker (2015)**: *Topic:* neighbourhood ties among fund managers. *Finding:* managers living near each other have more overlapping portfolios (only after one moves in), and these trades outperform by 6–7% per year.
- **Bailey, Cao, Kuchler, Stroebel & Wong (2018)**: *Topic:* the Social Connectedness Index (Facebook ties between regions). *Finding:* friendship links between places reflect geography and history (e.g., Dust Bowl migration).
- **Kuchler, Li, Peng, Stroebel & Zhou (2020)**: *Topic:* social proximity to capital. *Finding:* fund managers invest more in firms in socially connected regions but earn no higher returns, so the ties raise awareness without carrying information.
- **Maturana & Nickerson (2019)**: *Topic:* workplace peer effects among Texas teachers. *Finding:* teachers are more likely to refinance (often with the same bank) when colleagues with overlapping free periods do, which saves them money.
- **Kaustia & Knüpfer (2012)**: *Topic:* peer performance and stock market entry (Finland). *Finding:* strong local peer gains draw new investors in, but peer losses don't deter them, because losses aren't talked about.
- **Hvide & Östberg (2015)**: *Topic:* coworker influence on trading. *Finding:* coworkers strongly influence each other's stock picks, but copying them does not improve returns.
- **Chen, De, Hu & Hwang (2014); Bartov, Faurel & Mohanram (2018)**: *Topic:* social media opinions (Seeking Alpha, Twitter). *Finding:* the tone of posts predicts future returns and earnings, so the buzz reflects real information.
- **Cookson & Niessner (2020)**: *Topic:* StockTwits and self-labelled expertise. *Finding:* posts by self-described professionals predict positive returns over two months, while novice posts predict the opposite.
- **Bradley, Hanousek, Jame & Xiao (2024)**: *Topic:* WallStreetBets "Due Diligence" reports. *Finding:* DD posts used to predict returns, but stopped doing so after GameStop.
- **Cookson, Lu, Mullins & Niessner (2024)**: *Topic:* the social signal after GameStop. *Finding:* an influx of amateur users after GameStop drowned out the informative signal on StockTwits.
- **Tetlock, Saar-Tsechansky & Macskassy (2008)**: *Topic:* language in traditional media. *Finding:* traditional news leans negative, and its negative words predict firm fundamentals.
- **Cookson, Mullins & Niessner (2025)**: *Topic:* social media and finance (review). *Finding:* about 80% of social media messages about stocks are bullish.
- **Warkulat & Pelster (2024)**: *Topic:* WallStreetBets attention and retail trading. *Finding:* stocks bought during peak WSB attention lose an average of 8.5% by the time they are sold.
- **Cookson, Engelberg & Mullins (2023)**: *Topic:* echo chambers on StockTwits. *Finding:* users follow like-minded people (especially active traders), and acting on tips from narrow echo chambers loses up to 2.3% over five days.

#### Catching the Wave Before It Breaks

- **Bollen, Mao & Zeng (2011)**: *Topic:* Twitter mood and the stock market. *Finding:* the mood of tweets (calm vs. fearful) predicted Dow Jones moves a few days ahead with 87% accuracy.
- **Cookson, Lu, Mullins & Niessner (2024)**: *Topic:* attention vs. sentiment on social media. *Finding:* positive sentiment predicts higher next-day returns, while high attention causes a same-day spike that reverses the next day.

## Part II: Underreaction

### Chapter 5: Slow on the Uptake: When Markets Snooze on News

#### Opening: momentum

- **Jegadeesh & Titman (1993)**: *Topic:* momentum. *Finding:* buying 6-month winners and selling losers earned about 15% per year.
- **Jegadeesh & Titman (2001)**: *Topic:* momentum after publication. *Finding:* momentum returns eight years later were almost identical to those before publication.
- **Chen, Moskowitz & Shue (2016)**: *Topic:* the gambler's fallacy in high-stakes decisions. *Finding:* asylum judges, loan officers and baseball umpires tend to reverse their previous decisions after streaks.
- **Rouwenhorst (1998)**: *Topic:* international momentum. *Finding:* momentum is present in European stock markets.
- **Fama & French (2012)**: *Topic:* size, value and momentum internationally. *Finding:* momentum appears across regions, including Asia ex-Japan.
- **Rouwenhorst (1999)**: *Topic:* emerging market return factors. *Finding:* momentum appears in emerging markets such as Latin America.
- **Asness, Moskowitz & Pedersen (2013)**: *Topic:* momentum everywhere. *Finding:* momentum shows up in bonds, currencies and commodities as well as stocks.
- **Engelberg, Thompson & Williams**: *Topic:* anomalies in baseball cards. *Finding:* momentum also exists in baseball card prices.
- **Geczy & Samonov (2016)**: *Topic:* two centuries of momentum. *Finding:* momentum is present in US stocks back to 1801.
- **Chabot, Ghysels & Jagannathan**: *Topic:* momentum in Victorian England. *Finding:* momentum strategies were profitable in 19th-century British markets.
- **Goetzmann & Huang (2018)**: *Topic:* momentum in imperial Russia. *Finding:* momentum existed on the pre-revolution Russian stock market.

#### The Trend Is Your Friend – Everywhere

- **Moskowitz & Grinblatt (1999)**: *Topic:* industry momentum. *Finding:* buying the top-3 and selling the bottom-3 industries earns about the same as stock momentum, and stock momentum mostly disappears once industry effects are removed.
- **Moskowitz, Ooi & Pedersen (2012)**: *Topic:* time-series momentum (trend-following). *Finding:* a 12-month trend rule across 58 futures markets earned about 15% per year.
- **Hurst, Ooi & Pedersen (2017)**: *Topic:* a century of trend-following. *Finding:* time-series momentum works back to the 1880s.
- **Shynkevich (2016)**: *Topic:* wine market efficiency. *Finding:* momentum exists in wine but is too small to be profitable after costs.
- **Liu & Tsyvinski (2021)**: *Topic:* cryptocurrency risks and returns. *Finding:* last week's crypto market return predicts returns over the next 1–8 weeks (peaking around 4 weeks).

#### Second-Level Thinking, First-Class Returns

- **Hong, Lim & Stein (2000)**: *Topic:* momentum, firm size and analyst coverage. *Finding:* momentum is strongest in small, lightly covered stocks and among losers ("bad news travels slowly").

#### When Momentum Fails

- **Daniel & Moskowitz (2016)**: *Topic:* momentum crashes. *Finding:* crashes happen in market rebounds after bear markets with high volatility, and a dynamically timed strategy nearly doubles the Sharpe ratio (to about 1.2).
- **Lou & Polk (2022)**: *Topic:* comomentum (crowding in momentum). *Finding:* momentum earns 0.9% per month when crowding is low but −0.2% when it is high.

#### The Echo of Earnings

- **Bernard & Thomas (1989)**: *Topic:* post-earnings announcement drift (PEAD). *Finding:* buying the highest earnings surprises and selling the lowest earns 4.2% over 60 days.
- **Bernard & Thomas (1990)**: *Topic:* failure to anticipate future earnings implications. *Finding:* prices don't fully reflect what current earnings imply for future earnings, and the profits are largest just before the next announcement.
- **Hirshleifer, Lim & Teoh (2009)**: *Topic:* investor distraction. *Finding:* PEAD is much stronger (7.2% vs. 2.7%) on days when many other firms announce.
- **DellaVigna & Pollet (2009)**: *Topic:* Friday earnings announcements. *Finding:* the immediate reaction to Friday announcements is 15% smaller and the later drift 70% larger.
- **Womack (1996)**: *Topic:* analyst recommendations. *Finding:* prices keep drifting after analyst upgrades and downgrades.
- **Michaely, Thaler & Womack (1995)**: *Topic:* dividend initiations and omissions. *Finding:* prices keep drifting in the same direction after dividend changes.
- **Ikenberry, Lakonishok & Vermaelen (1995)**: *Topic:* open-market share repurchases. *Finding:* the market underreacts to buyback announcements, and prices drift up afterwards.
- **Manconi, Peyer & Vermaelen (2019)**: *Topic:* buybacks around the world. *Finding:* the buyback underreaction also holds internationally.
- **Loughran & Ritter (1995)**: *Topic:* the new issues puzzle. *Finding:* firms that issue shares underperform over the long run.

#### Missed Connections

- **Cohen & Frazzini (2008)**: *Topic:* customer–supplier economic links. *Finding:* only 60% of a customer's shock is priced into its supplier on day one, and trading on this earns 1.3% per month (strongest when no fund owns both firms).

#### A Hard Act to Follow

- **Pepitone & DiNubile (1976)**: *Topic:* contrast effects in judging crimes. *Finding:* people judge a burglary as less serious after reading about a murder.
- **Kenrick & Gutierres (1980)**: *Topic:* contrast effects in judging attractiveness. *Finding:* people are rated as less attractive after viewers see images of very attractive people.
- **Hartzmark & Shue (2018)**: *Topic:* contrast effects in financial markets. *Finding:* the better yesterday's earnings announcements, the lower today's announcers' returns, and trading on this earns about 15% per year.

#### Lazy Prices, Loud Signals

- **Cohen, Malloy & Nguyen (2020)**: *Topic:* "lazy prices" (changes in 10-K text). *Finding:* shorting firms that change their 10-Ks a lot and buying non-changers earns 7% per year (22% for Risk Factors changes), and 86% of changes are negative.

#### The Sound of Silence

- **Giglio & Shue (2014)**: *Topic:* "no news is news" in mergers. *Finding:* the odds of a deal completing rise as time passes, but target prices don't adjust, so buying late-stage targets earns about 9% more per year.

### Chapter 6: The Hidden Goldmine: Why Intangibles Are the Market's Blind Spot

#### Satisfaction Guaranteed

- **Edmans (2011, 2012)**: *Topic:* employee satisfaction (Fortune 100 Best Companies to Work For) and stock returns. *Finding:* Best Companies beat peers by 2.3–3.8% per year (1984–2011), the market took 4–5 years to price this in, and they systematically beat earnings forecasts.
- **Boustanifar & Kang (2022)**: *Topic:* replication of employee satisfaction and returns, 1984–2020. *Finding:* Best Companies kept outperforming after publication.
- **Green, Huang, Wen & Zhou (2019)**: *Topic:* Glassdoor employer reviews. *Finding:* improvements in crowdsourced employee ratings predict higher stock returns.
- **Sparkline Capital (2021, practitioner study)**: *Topic:* measuring culture with NLP on Glassdoor reviews. *Finding:* firms with innovative cultures beat rivals by about 2% per year, while toxic cultures lag by a similar margin.

#### The Fluff that Pays

- **Gompers, Ishii & Metrick (2003)**: *Topic:* corporate governance and equity prices. *Finding:* well-governed firms (strong shareholder rights) beat poorly governed ones by 8.5% per year.
- **Fornell, Mithas, Morgeson & Krishnan (2006)**: *Topic:* customer satisfaction (ACSI) and stock prices. *Finding:* top-20% ACSI firms delivered double the Dow's return (1997–2003) at lower risk.
- **Fornell, Morgeson & Hult (2016)**: *Topic:* customer satisfaction, revisited. *Finding:* the customer satisfaction premium was still there a decade later.
- **Huang (2018)**: *Topic:* consumer opinions (14.5 million Amazon reviews). *Finding:* buying firms with highly rated products and shorting poorly rated ones earned 7–9% per year.
- **Boustanifar & Kang (2025)**: *Topic:* the brand premium (Interbrand values; "brand" mentions in reports). *Finding:* strong-brand firms beat peers by about 3% per year, more so when the brand is built rather than bought.

#### Beyond the Sale

- **Kang, Stice-Lawrence & Wong (2021)**: *Topic:* satellite images of store parking lots and local information. *Finding:* local fund managers trade on nearby car-park traffic and earn about 2% per year in abnormal returns.
- **Yu, Hao, Wu, Zhao & Wang (2023)**: *Topic:* satellite images of container ports. *Finding:* busier ports predict national stock returns, and a timing strategy earns about 16% per year.
- **Wiles & Danielova (2009)**: *Topic:* product placement in films. *Finding:* featured firms' stocks rise about 1% on the film's opening day, even though tie-ins were announced months earlier.

#### Dirty Money

- **Bolton & Kacperczyk (2021)**: *Topic:* carbon emissions and stock returns. *Finding:* firms with higher carbon emissions earn higher returns, which the authors read as a carbon risk premium.
- **Atilgan, Demirtas, Edmans & Gunaydin (2023)**: *Topic:* whether the carbon premium is risk or outperformance. *Finding:* heavy emitters regularly beat earnings forecasts, which explains up to 40% of their outperformance, so the premium reflects mispricing rather than risk.

#### Moneyball for Ideas

- **Chan, Lakonishok & Sougiannis (2001)**: *Topic:* market valuation of R&D. *Finding:* firms with high accumulated R&D (relative to market value) beat the market by about 6% per year.
- **Eberhart, Maxwell & Siddique (2004)**: *Topic:* R&D increases. *Finding:* firms with sharp R&D increases earn abnormal returns of 3–5% value-weighted and 7–8% equal-weighted.
- **Cohen, Diether & Malloy (2013)**: *Topic:* "misvaluing innovation" (R&D ability). *Finding:* buying high R&D spenders with a record of turning R&D into sales, and selling poor converters, earns about 11% per year.
- **Hirshleifer, Hsu & Li (2013)**: *Topic:* innovative efficiency (patent citations per R&D dollar). *Finding:* efficient innovators beat wasteful spenders by 10–11% per year.
- **Hirshleifer, Hsu & Li (2018)**: *Topic:* innovative originality. *Finding:* firms whose patents draw on many technological fields outperform the least original by 3–4% per year.

#### Extracting More Value

- **Lev & Srivastava (2022)**: *Topic:* explaining the failure of value investing. *Finding:* capitalising R&D and parts of SG&A reshuffles about half of value/glamour stocks, and the adjusted value strategy beat the old one in 34 of 49 years.
- **Sparkline Capital (2021, practitioner study)**: *Topic:* "intangible value" built with AI from patents, job postings, reviews, etc. *Finding:* $1 in 1995 grew to $29 by 2021, against $13 for the S&P 500 and $14 for a growth index.

#### From Man vs Machine to Man + Machine

- **Lopez-Lira & Tang**: *Topic:* ChatGPT and stock return prediction. *Finding:* trading on ChatGPT's positive/negative ratings of news headlines earned 0.38% per day (Oct 2021–Dec 2023, before costs).
- **Cao, Jiang, Wang & Yang (2024)**: *Topic:* AI analysts vs. human analysts. *Finding:* AI beats humans on clear, structured information, humans do better on small, intangible-heavy or distressed firms, and the combination does best.
- **Cohen, Lu & Nguyen (2026)**: *Topic:* "mimicking finance" (predicting fund manager trades with a neural network). *Finding:* AI predicts about 71% of managers' trades, and the unpredictable 29% are the ones most associated with outperformance.

### Chapter 7: C-Suite Secrets: Signals from the Summit

#### Opening: AGMs, earnings calls and CEO tells

- **Li & Yermack (2016)**: *Topic:* evasive shareholder meetings (remote AGM locations). *Finding:* firms that make a one-time switch to a far-away AGM venue underperform by 11.7% over six months, and remote venues in general by 6.8%.
- **Cohen, Lou & Malloy (2020)**: *Topic:* "casting" conference calls (choosing which analysts ask questions). *Finding:* selling firms that call on bullish analysts and buying those that don't earns 1.5% per month (19% per year), internationally too.
- **Mayew & Venkatachalam (2012)**: *Topic:* vocal emotion of CEOs on earnings calls. *Finding:* genuinely upbeat voices predict better results, while stressed voices (cognitive dissonance) predict underperformance of 6.5% over six months that the market ignores.
- **Dikolli, Keusch, Mayew & Steffen (2020)**: *Topic:* causal language in CEO shareholder letters (behavioural integrity). *Finding:* letters heavy with "because"/"therefore" predict underperformance and earnings restatements.

#### Follow the Leader – Unless She's on Autopilot

- **Cohen, Malloy & Pomorski (2012)**: *Topic:* decoding insider trades (routine vs. opportunistic). *Finding:* copying only opportunistic (off-pattern) insider trades earns over 21% per year equal-weighted (about 10% value-weighted), while routine trades carry no information.

#### Flights of Fancy

- **Yermack (2006)**: *Topic:* corporate jets and CEO perks. *Finding:* firms that let CEOs use company jets personally underperform by about 4% per year.

#### The Deal that Keeps on Taking

- **Agrawal, Jaffe & Mandelker (1992)**: *Topic:* post-merger performance of acquirers. *Finding:* acquirers underperform by about 10% over the five years after completion.
- **Loughran & Vijh (1997)**: *Topic:* long-run returns by deal type and payment method. *Finding:* stock-financed mergers underperform by about 25% over five years, while cash tender offers outperform.
- **Cooper, Gulen & Schill (2008)**: *Topic:* the asset growth anomaly. *Finding:* buying low-asset-growth firms and shorting high-asset-growth firms earns about 8% per year.

#### House of Cards

- **Liu & Yermack (2012)**: *Topic:* CEOs' home purchases. *Finding:* CEOs who buy very large homes see 7–9% underperformance the next year, worse when the purchase is financed by selling company stock.

#### The Perils of the Pedestal

- **Malmendier & Tate (2009)**: *Topic:* superstar CEOs (media awards). *Finding:* award-winning CEOs get distracted (books, boards, golf), manage earnings and get pay rises, and their firms underperform similar non-winners by 15–26% over three years.

#### Narcissism Is a Bad Sign

- **Ham, Lang & Seybert (2017)**: *Topic:* CFO narcissism (signature size) and reporting quality. *Finding:* bigger signatures correlate with higher narcissism scores, and narcissistic CFOs produce lower-quality financial reports.
- **Ham, Seybert & Wang (2018)**: *Topic:* CEO signature size, investment and performance. *Finding:* big-signature people lie more in lab games, and big-signature CEOs overinvest in flashy projects, neglect maintenance and deliver lower profitability.
- **Chatterjee & Hambrick (2007)**: *Topic:* narcissistic CEOs (photo size, "I" vs "we", press mentions, pay gap). *Finding:* narcissistic CEOs pursue bolder strategies that produce more extreme, not better, performance.
- **Jia, van Lent & Zeng (2014)**: *Topic:* facial masculinity (width-to-height ratio) and misreporting. *Finding:* CEOs with wider faces are more likely to misreport earnings and are three times more likely to face SEC fraud investigations.

#### Skin in the Game

- **Coles, Daniel & Naveen (2024)**: *Topic:* Musk's $56 billion Tesla pay package. *Finding:* the package carried extremely demanding performance targets, which Tesla met, so it rewarded value creation.
- **von Lilienfeld-Toal & Ruenzi (2014)**: *Topic:* CEO ownership and stock returns. *Finding:* buying firms with high CEO ownership earns 4–10% per year, more so where other governance is weak.

#### Cashing In Before Cashing Out

- **Edmans, Fang & Huang (2022)**: *Topic:* short-term incentives (equity vesting) and buybacks. *Finding:* CEOs time buybacks around their equity vesting to lift the price before selling, which gives a small short-term gain and a 1.0–1.6% decline in the second year.

## Part III: Misreaction

### Chapter 8: When 2 + 2 = 5: Adding Up Market Mistakes

#### Opening: Palm and 3Com

- **Lamont & Thaler (2003)**: *Topic:* mispricing in tech-stock carve-outs (Palm/3Com). *Finding:* in six carve-outs from 1998–2000, the market valued the subsidiary above its parent (negative "stub" values) for months, because short-sale constraints blocked arbitrage.

#### The Law that Finance Forgot

- **Musto, Nini & Schwarz (2018)**: *Topic:* on-the-run vs. off-the-run Treasuries in the financial crisis. *Finding:* an old 30-year bond with the same cash flows as a new 10-year note yielded 4.14% vs. 3.78% in November 2008.
- **Krishnamurthy (2002)**: *Topic:* the bond/old-bond spread. *Finding:* older (off-the-run) Treasuries consistently trade cheaper than otherwise identical new ones, reflecting a liquidity premium.
- **Chaboud, Correia Golay, Fleming, Huh, Keane & Shachar (2025)**: *Topic:* liquidity in off-the-run Treasuries. *Finding:* the on/off-the-run gap spikes in stress episodes (2008, COVID-19, the 2025 tariffs).
- *(Rating-agency default data, Moody's / S&P; ICE BofA indices)*: *Topic:* the investment-grade cutoff (BBB− vs. BB+). *Finding:* BB+ bonds default only about 0.25% per year more than BBB− bonds, yet yield about 1.5% more, roughly ten times the fair compensation.
- **Makarov & Schoar (2020)**: *Topic:* arbitrage across crypto exchanges ("kimchi premium"). *Finding:* across 34 exchanges in 19 countries, Bitcoin traded at persistent premia outside the US and Europe, only partly explained by capital controls.

#### The Case of the Mismatched Twins

- **De Jong, Rosenthal & Van Dijk (2009)**: *Topic:* dual-listed companies (e.g., Royal Dutch/Shell). *Finding:* twin shares deviate from parity for long periods, so arbitrage is risky (as LTCM found), but buying the cheaper twin pays off.

#### Discount Wars

- **Lee, Shleifer & Thaler (1991)**: *Topic:* the closed-end fund puzzle. *Finding:* closed-end fund discounts move with retail investor sentiment.
- **Bradley, Brav, Goldstein & Jiang (2010)**: *Topic:* activist campaigns to open-end closed-end funds. *Finding:* activists target the deepest discounts, and their campaigns halve the discounts on average.

#### Gold Medals for All

- **Arnott, Cornell & Wu (2021, Research Affiliates)**: *Topic:* the big market delusion in electric vehicles. *Finding:* the EV sector was valued at $1.5 trillion (above the whole traditional auto industry) despite about 1/42 of its sales.
- **Cornell & Damodaran (2020)**: *Topic:* the big market delusion. *Finding:* when a sector is hyped, investors value every firm as if it will be the winner, so the sector as a whole is overvalued (dot-com, cannabis, EVs).

#### Means Without Meaning

- **Bessembinder (2018)**: *Topic:* do stocks outperform Treasury bills? *Finding:* fewer than 3 in 7 US stocks beat T-bills over their lifetimes, the most common outcome is −100%, and 4% of firms account for all net wealth creation.

#### It's All Relative

- *(Blender vs. TV experiment, authors not named)*: *Topic:* relative vs. absolute thinking. *Finding:* people will travel to save $45 on a $50 blender but not on a $1,000 TV.
- **Shue & Townsend (2021)**: *Topic:* non-proportional thinking and nominal share prices. *Finding:* investors think in dollar moves, so doubling a stock's price lowers its volatility by 20–30%.
- **Kumar (2009)**: *Topic:* who gambles in the stock market (lottery-type/penny stocks). *Finding:* investors treat cheap stocks like lottery tickets and bid them up, and they then underperform.
- **Hartzmark & Shue**: *Topic:* counterproductive sustainable investing (impact elasticity). *Finding:* cheaper capital cuts absolute emissions far more at brown firms than at green ones.

### Chapter 9: Misguided Missiles: Locking on to the Wrong Targets

#### Opening: the disposition effect

- **Shefrin & Statman (1985)**: *Topic:* the disposition effect. *Finding:* investors are about 1.5 times as likely to sell winners as losers, regardless of holding period.
- **Odean (1998)**: *Topic:* reluctance to realise losses. *Finding:* investors realise 14.8% of gains vs. 9.8% of losses, and the winners they sell beat the losers they keep by 3% (4.4% after tax).

#### Bias in the Big Leagues

- **Genesove & Mayer (2001)**: *Topic:* loss aversion in housing. *Finding:* home sellers facing nominal losses set higher asking prices and wait longer to sell.
- **Beggs & Graddy (2009)**: *Topic:* anchoring in art auctions. *Finding:* art buyers and sellers anchor on past prices.
- **List (2004)**: *Topic:* prospect theory in sports card markets. *Finding:* inexperienced sports-card traders show endowment/loss-aversion effects that fade with market experience.
- **Schatzmann & Haslhofer (2023)**: *Topic:* the disposition effect in Bitcoin. *Finding:* Bitcoin investors also sell winners and hold losers.
- **Huang & Goetzmann (2023)**: *Topic:* selection neglect in the NFT bubble. *Finding:* the disposition effect is also present among NFT traders.
- **Frazzini (2006)**: *Topic:* the disposition effect among fund managers and underreaction. *Finding:* fund managers are 1.7 times as likely to sell winners, which causes post-earnings drift, and a strategy using unrealised gains earns 2.4% per month.

#### The Psychology Behind the Sell

- **Kahneman & Tversky (1979)**: *Topic:* prospect theory. *Finding:* people are risk-averse over gains and risk-seeking over losses (loss aversion).
- **Barberis & Xiong (2012)**: *Topic:* realisation utility. *Finding:* a loss-averse investor would actually buy after gains and sell after losses, so the disposition effect is better explained by the pleasure/pain of *realising* gains and losses.

#### Rolling Over the Pain

- **Frydman, Hartzmark & Solomon (2018)**: *Topic:* rolling mental accounts. *Finding:* the disposition effect disappears when sale proceeds are immediately reinvested, and these investors' sold stocks underperform by about 5% more over two years.

#### The High that Holds You Back

- **George & Hwang (2004)**: *Topic:* the 52-week high and momentum. *Finding:* buying stocks near their 52-week high and selling those far below it earns 1.23% per month, with no long-run reversal.
- **Bali, Cakici & Whitelaw (2011)**: *Topic:* stocks as lotteries (MAX effect). *Finding:* selling stocks with the biggest one-day gains in the past month and buying those with the smallest earns about 12% per year.
- **Kumar (2009)**: *Topic:* lottery-like stocks. *Finding:* stocks with skewed, lottery-like return distributions underperform.

#### The Madness of Markers: When £100 Isn't Just £100

- **Bhattacharya, Holden & Jacobsen (2012)**: *Topic:* buy–sell imbalances around round numbers. *Finding:* traders buy just below and sell just above round prices, which costs investors about $813 million a year.
- **Donaldson & Kim (1993)**: *Topic:* price barriers in the Dow Jones. *Finding:* round index levels act as resistance, but once broken the index moves further and faster.

#### What You See Is Not What You Get

- **Hartzmark & Solomon (2019)**: *Topic:* the dividend disconnect (free dividends fallacy). *Finding:* investors and analysts treat dividends as free money (analysts overestimate future prices by about the dividend yield), and the disposition effect depends only on price changes.
- **Hartzmark & Solomon (2013)**: *Topic:* the dividend month premium. *Finding:* stocks expected to pay a dividend in a given month outperform (4.4% per year value-weighted), mostly before the ex-date.
- **Hartzmark & Solomon (2022)**: *Topic:* reconsidering returns (mutual fund flows). *Finding:* fund flows respond to NAV price changes rather than total returns.
- **Hartzmark & Solomon (2025)**: *Topic:* market-wide predictable price pressure from dividend reinvestment. *Finding:* investors rarely reinvest dividends in the paying stock, and timing the market around big payout dates raises returns from 7.2% to 8.7% per year.

### Chapter 10: Legends in Our Own Minds: The Overconfidence Trap

#### Opening: overconfidence

- **Svenson (1981)**: *Topic:* overconfidence in driving ability. *Finding:* most people (about 90%) rate themselves as above-average drivers.

#### The Bias Behind the Boast

- **Gervais & Odean (2001)**: *Topic:* learning to be overconfident (self-attribution). *Finding:* successful traders credit themselves for wins, so overconfidence grows with experience.
- **Odean (1999)**: *Topic:* do investors trade too much? *Finding:* stocks investors buy in "swap" trades underperform the ones they sell by about 3% per year, even before costs.
- **Barber & Odean (2000)**: *Topic:* trading is hazardous to your wealth. *Finding:* retail investors earn 16.7% after costs vs. 17.9% for the market, and the most active traders earn only 11.4%.

#### Boys Will Be Boys

- **Lundeberg, Fox & Puncochar (1994)**: *Topic:* gender and confidence. *Finding:* men are more confident than women even when wrong, with similar accuracy.
- **Deaux & Farris (1977)**: *Topic:* gender and attribution of performance. *Finding:* men attribute success to skill and failure to luck, while women do the reverse.
- **Deaux & Emswiller (1974)**: *Topic:* sex-linked tasks. *Finding:* the same performance is seen as skill for men and luck for women on "masculine" tasks.
- **Lenney (1977)**: *Topic:* women's self-confidence. *Finding:* gender gaps in confidence are largest when feedback is ambiguous.
- **Barber & Odean (2001)**: *Topic:* gender, overconfidence and investing. *Finding:* men trade 45% more than women and earn about 1% less per year, and single men do worst.

#### Folly at Your Fingertips

- **Barber & Odean (2002)**: *Topic:* switching from phone to online trading. *Finding:* after going online, investors who had previously beaten the market traded more and underperformed by more than 3% per year.
- **Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz (2022)**: *Topic:* attention-induced trading on Robinhood. *Finding:* Robinhood users herd into attention stocks, and the most-bought stocks fall about 5% over 20 days (nearly 20% on intense days).
- **Ivo Welch** *(named in text; the endnote reads "Ibid.")*: *Topic:* Robinhood users' aggregate holdings. *Finding:* a portfolio mirroring Robinhood holdings beat the market by 1.3% per month in 2018–2020.

#### Shiny Toys, Bleeding Accounts

- **Cornelli, Doerr, Frost & Gambacorta (2023, BIS)**: *Topic:* retail losses in crypto. *Finding:* most retail Bitcoin investors in 95 countries lost money, because they bought about two months after price surges while large holders sold.
- **Black & Scholes (1973)**: *Topic:* option pricing. *Finding:* derives the option pricing formula, whose assumptions often fail in practice.
- **Bryzgalova, Pavlova & Sikorskaya (2023)**: *Topic:* retail options trading. *Finding:* retail options traders lost $2.1 billion (28–40 cents per $100 traded), mainly through wide bid–ask spreads (12.6%, or 23.5% on small trades).

#### All In, All Gone

- **Evans & Archer (1968); Statman (1987)**: *Topic:* how many stocks make a diversified portfolio. *Finding:* you need about 20 well-chosen stocks for most diversification benefits and 30–40 for the full gains.
- **Barber & Odean** *(cited in text)*: *Topic:* retail portfolio concentration. *Finding:* the average retail investor holds just four stocks.
- **Vanguard, *How America Saves 2025***: *Topic:* employer stock in 401(k)s. *Finding:* one in four workers with access invests at least 20% of savings in company stock.
- **French & Poterba (1991)**: *Topic:* home bias. *Finding:* investors heavily overweight domestic stocks.
- **Huberman (2001)**: *Topic:* familiarity breeds investment. *Finding:* investors prefer firms headquartered near them.
- **Sharpe (1987)**: *Topic:* asset allocation and fixed-mix strategies. *Finding:* maintaining a constant stock/bond mix beats letting the allocation drift.
- **Merton (1969)**: *Topic:* lifetime portfolio selection. *Finding:* the optimal equity share varies over the life cycle (more equities when young, because human capital acts like a bond).

## Part IV: Correction

### Chapter 11: Outsmarting the Market: Turning Madness into Opportunity

#### 12. Play the Game

- **Mankiw & Zeldes (1991); Menkhoff & Westermann**: *Topic:* the stock market participation puzzle. *Finding:* surprisingly few households invest in stocks at all.
- **Mehra & Prescott (1985)**: *Topic:* the equity premium puzzle. *Finding:* stocks' excess return over safe assets is too large to be explained by standard risk aversion.
- **Thaler & Benartzi (2004)**: *Topic:* Save More Tomorrow. *Finding:* committing future pay rises to savings sharply increases employee saving rates.

#### 13. Count the Hidden Costs

- **Barber, Odean & Zheng (2005)**: *Topic:* fund expenses and flows. *Finding:* investors respond to visible front-end loads but barely to less visible ongoing expense ratios.

#### 16. Sit on Your Hands

- **Benartzi & Thaler (1995)**: *Topic:* myopic loss aversion. *Finding:* investors who evaluate portfolios less often take more equity risk and earn more, because they react less to short-term losses.

#### 18. Insure What Matters – and Only That

- *(UK population and insurance data, author's calculation)*: *Topic:* income protection vs. life insurance. *Finding:* you are about 4–5 times more likely to suffer long-term illness that stops you working than to die young.
